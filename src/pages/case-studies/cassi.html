Background

A new problem to solve

After delivering the main updates for i-Exchange, I was on the hunt for new problems to solve. While conducting user research with a frontline colleague, I noticed they were using a tool called Colleague Assist.

Colleague Assist is an AI chatbot that provides Santander colleagues with guidance on how to serve customers. It's a bit like ChatGPT for banking. It uses i-Exchange, Santander's internal knowledge base, as its source of information to provide answers.

Joining the team

After speaking to the Product Owner, I found out they were having trouble getting users to select their business area, or audience, and only a fraction of responses were receiving feedback. They were happy for me to join the team. However, there was a big problem. There was no money left.

Developers agreed that front end changes such as copy and styles would count as business as usual and not incur additional costs. That helped, but it limited my options. There was also a long change freeze coming up in three weeks, so it was a race against time.

Do what you can

Functional changes in the back end were out of the question, so I focused on what we could do. My knowledge of front end development was enough to understand the low cost changes I could make, so I focused on the UI instead of trying to create new journeys or improve the quality of responses directly.

In a bid to drive engagement with colleagues, I also announced a competition to design a new logo and posted a banner on i-Exchange to promote it.

Empathise

There's always time for research

I wanted to understand why users weren't giving feedback or selecting their audience. By putting a town hall meeting in place, I quickly understood user needs and learned more about their pain points.

Town hall meeting

  1. Feedback

    There were only three feedback options, which did not always describe the user's thoughts. These discrete options were potentially causing high drop off rates.

  2. Understanding

    Users were not aware that the model learns directly from their feedback. Once this was clear, they felt far more inclined to leave feedback.

  3. Audiences

    The audience selection menu was at the top of the screen, but the query was entered at the bottom. There were no prompts to select audience, so users often forgot.

Heuristic analysis

  • No guidance

    It was not clear that users should select an audience or how important it was to give feedback.

  • Simple errors

    The audience selection was out of place, the feedback icons were wedged between responses, and don't get me started on colour contrast.

  • Dated UI

    Internal projects never seem to get UI designers involved, and it showed. The tool was in pilot, but it only needed a few tweaks to improve it.

  • Unclear feedback journey

    The icons were there, but users were not clicking them. The start of the feedback journey was not clear enough.

The thumbs up and down controls existed, but they were visually quiet and appeared between responses with no explanation of why feedback mattered.

The problem was not only the feedback form. It was the complete lack of signalling around the start of the journey.

Define

Three problems to solve

Colleagues are not selecting their business area (audience) when querying Colleague Assist and the feedback rates are really low. This causes Colleague Assist's responses to be less accurate and prevents the data science team from improving the model.

The feedback problem

Users wanted to leave feedback about why the chatbot was wrong, but they only had three options which were too broad. This meant they did not get to share their true thoughts and feelings, so they dropped off the journey.

Cassi uses feedback to improve its responses, guard railing those that receive multiple thumbs down. This is really important to reduce risk, so feedback is vital. I needed to look at how we received feedback to inform the model. The thumbs up and down were also not clearly signalled, so people were likely missing the start of the journey.

The understanding problem

Users did not have a good mental model of Colleague Assist. They did not understand the value in reviewing responses or realise that this could improve the model for their benefit. It was obvious from the town hall meeting that there was a knowledge gap I needed to address to get buy in from users.

The audience problem

Users were not selecting their audience regularly enough. This meant responses could be guard railed for being wrong when really they were just shown to the wrong audience. The layout did not make sense, there were no prompts to choose an audience and no guidance for new users. There needed to be a clearer journey and guidance for users to select their audience. Audience selection should become second nature, as all queries should be filtered by audience.

Ideate

Three days to hand over designs

I was riding solo for this ideation phase. There were only three days before I needed to hand over designs to developers, so I had to move quickly and get mock ups ready super fast.

If only I had somewhere users could leave their feedback

I had built a good relationship with the Community Forum team, so I asked about creating a new feedback forum for Colleague Assist. They said yes. Community moderators would monitor the forum and triage feedback to the Colleague Assist team. The discrete feedback options were gone, and even better, the Community forum was free.

Plugging the knowledge gap

I noticed a complete lack of guidance on the tool. When colleagues were onboarded they were given training, but due to knowledge fade some might not recall the basics. Tooltips are commonly used to provide context where a user needs support, so I used one to keep things concise and provide key information.

When users first opened the tool there was a big empty space in the window. I used it to provide some initial guidance.

Guidance without a new journey

With no budget for back end changes, I used the opening state and a tooltip to explain how to query Cassi, why audience mattered, and how feedback improved the model.

The guidance had to be visible for new users, but not heavy enough to slow down experienced colleagues.

What about the audience filters?

The audience filter menu was positioned at the top of the screen, far away from where users entered their query. Would moving it closer be enough? The law of proximity would indicate yes, so I tried it out.

A new name and logo

Alongside the product fixes, I ran a competition to design a new logo and give the tool a new name. It helped promote the tool, drive engagement and make the product feel more mature.

Test

Rapid validation on the final day

I was on my final day before handover, so I quickly put in some group calls with users to check my design choices. Users thought it was a big upgrade and they really liked the new option for feedback.

Last minute changes

Colleague Assist was always a web based tool, but I learned that many users minimised their screen to take up a slim column on the right hand side. This meant they could use other windows while serving customers.

The screen needed a smaller breakpoint. By reducing the size of the logo in the top bar and reducing spacing lower down, there was space to fit the chat window. I made sure larger screens were not impacted, but it went down to the wire.

Designing for the window users actually used

The testing sessions revealed that colleagues often kept Cassi in a narrow side panel while serving customers. That changed the responsive requirement at the last moment.

This was not a mobile breakpoint. It was a live-work breakpoint.

Toast or overlay?

I created two ways of displaying the feedback journey. One was an overlay that the user would need to dismiss. The other was a toast that would appear for 10 seconds at the top of the chat window.

Users were far more keen on the toast. They made an excellent point that they would usually be speaking to a customer live, so overlay pop ups would get in the way of providing good service.

Toast it is.

Toast over overlay

The toast gave confirmation without blocking the chat. That mattered because users were often in the middle of a live customer interaction.

The less prominent pattern was the more useful one.

Final product

Cassi

  • Audience

    Moving the audience field and adding a prompt made it much clearer that it needed to be selected.

  • Tooltip

    A simple tooltip held guidance for new users without getting in the way of colleagues who knew what they were doing.

  • Feedback

    Users had a clearer path to mark responses and share their thoughts. The new toast went down a treat.

  • Rebrand

    By engaging with our community, we had a new logo, new name and better brand for Cassi.

Results

Right on time

  • ~100%

    Audience adherence

    In the first month following deployment, every guard railed response had an audience selected.

  • +30%

    Feedback responses

    The clearer feedback journey and new forum increased feedback engagement.

  • £0

    Additional cost

    Front end changes were delivered as business as usual before the change freeze.

Did I deliver in three weeks?

Right on time. The changes were delivered just before the change freeze and received plenty of positive feedback on the new Community forum page. A month after release, audience selections were close to 100% adherence and the feedback count was higher than ever. Best of all, I achieved this with no additional development cost.

Community support

By providing a dedicated forum for colleagues, they started sharing their thoughts and we were able to increase feedback engagement. Experienced colleagues were helping new starters and we gained more context for bad responses. Often, the feedback was about a process on i-Exchange, so i-Exchange managers had the information they needed to improve processes.

The message was clear

The new thumbs up and down UI clearly signalled the start of the feedback journey. The tooltip and toast provided colleagues with the guidance they needed. We also linked up with training teams to communicate this further. Combined with the new forum, these changes meant that feedback rates increased by 30%.

Adherence improved

Prior to the update, 1 in 3 guard railed responses did not have an audience selected. In the first month following Cassi's deployment, there were 30 guard railed responses. In all cases, the user had selected their audience. This was a marked improvement and meant the team were spending more time reviewing the responses that really needed their attention.

Cassi was born

The competition to design a new logo went down a hit. We received dozens of submissions and even a new name suggestion. The designs were shown to a panel of judges.

Aishat Arowosegbe won the competition by designing the Cassi logo.

Retrospective

What I'd do differently

  • Being adaptable

    This project highlighted that you can still create great products without going by the book. I would have preferred comprehensive research and better testing, but constraints often prevent this. Having the adaptability to go off track to deliver value sooner was the right choice here.

  • Make everything responsive

    I assumed I was building for a web screen. That was a bad assumption. Finding out about the minimised view so close to handover was almost a big problem. This taught me to set up designs to be responsive for web, tablet, mobile and narrow working views.

  • Putting my bias aside

    I had my own thoughts about the overlay versus toast decision. I would have preferred the overlay, but when colleagues made it clear that it could hamper their ability to serve a customer, it was an easy choice.
